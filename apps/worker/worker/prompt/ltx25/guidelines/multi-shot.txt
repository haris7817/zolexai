LTX-2.5 MULTI-SHOT RULES

USE WHEN
Use only when the user wants actual edited shots rather than one camera move. Prefer two to four connected shots in a single generation.

OUTPUT SHAPE
Write one chronological paragraph or a short connected prose sequence. Do not output a numbered shot list. Do not rely on screenplay sluglines alone to communicate edits.

OPENING SHOT
Establish the first shot with its scale, angle, subject placement, setting, lighting, action, and active soundscape.

EVERY CUT MUST INCLUDE
1. An explicit transition stated naturally, for example: "A hard cut transitions to...", "The view cuts to...", "A match cut connects...", or "The image dissolves into..."
2. The new shot scale and camera angle.
3. Who or what appears in frame and where it is positioned.
4. The setting and any intentional lighting change.
5. The same concise visual anchors for every recurring person or object.
6. A direct statement that music, dialogue, effects, and ambience continue, change, fade, drop, or stop.

SHOT DESIGN
Give each shot one clear purpose, such as establishing the geography, showing a decisive action, revealing a detail, or capturing a reaction. Keep every action chronological and readable. Re-establish the recurring subject after each cut instead of relying only on pronouns.

CONTINUITY
Preserve character identity, body characteristics, wardrobe, object appearance, scene geography, lighting logic, visual style, and voice across cuts unless the prompt explicitly establishes a motivated change of time or place. Audio continuity must be described at every cut.

CAMERA MOVEMENT VERSUS EDITING
"The camera pans/tracks/circles/pushes" continues the current shot. "A hard cut/match cut/dissolve transitions" begins another shot. Never use movement language as a substitute for an edit, and never describe an edit as if it were continuous camera travel.

REJECT OR REWRITE WHEN
- More than four shots are requested without a strong reason.
- A cut is implied but not named.
- A new shot fails to re-establish framing or the recurring subject.
- Audio continuity is missing at a cut.
- Clothing, identity, lighting, geography, visual style, or voice changes without explanation.
- Several competing actions or camera instructions occur simultaneously.
